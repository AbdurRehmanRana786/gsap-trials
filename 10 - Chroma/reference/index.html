Chroma Room 2 — Five pigments that changed everything

Era 01 · The Palaeolithic

40,000 BC

Ochre Fe₂O₃ · iron-oxide earth

The first colour was dirt. Dug from iron-rich clay, ground on a stone and blown through a hollow bone, red ochre left the oldest signatures we have: a hand pressed to a cave wall, saying I was here.

Source
Iron-rich clay
Found at
Sulawesi, Blombos
Cost
Free, if you could find it

Era 02 · Bronze-age Phoenicia

1500 BC

Tyrian Purple C₁₆H₈Br₂N₂O₂ · dibromo-indigo

Ten thousand murex sea snails gave their lives for a single gram. The dyers of Tyre steeped them for days — the stench was legendary, and so was the price. For centuries only emperors were allowed to wear it.

Source
Murex sea snails
Yield
1 g per 10,000 shells
Worn by
Emperors only

Era 03 · Medieval Europe

1200s

Ultramarine Na₇Al₆Si₆O₂₄S₃ · lazurite

Ultramarinus — from beyond the sea. Ground from lapis lazuli mined in a single Afghan valley and shipped to Venice, it cost more than gold. Painters saved it for the Virgin’s robe, and contracts named the ounces.

Source
Lapis lazuli, Sar-e-Sang
Price
More than gold
Saved for
The Virgin’s robe

Era 04 · The Enlightenment

1706

Prussian Blue Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃ · ferric ferrocyanide

A Berlin colour-maker was trying to make red. A contaminated batch of potash turned his pot a deep, steady blue instead — the first modern synthetic pigment. A century later, Hokusai painted a great wave with it.

Source
A happy accident
Made by
J. J. Diesbach
Famous for
The Great Wave

Era 05 · The Industrial Age

1856

Mauveine C₂₇H₂₅N₄⁺ · aniline dye

Eighteen-year-old William Perkin was trying to make quinine in a home lab over the Easter holidays. He got a purple sludge instead — the first synthetic dye. Within a decade London had “mauve measles”, and colour belonged to everyone.

Source
Coal tar
Made by
W. H. Perkin, aged 18
Result
Mauve measles
